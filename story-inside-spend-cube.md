# Method: The story inside the spend cube

*Drafted: May 2026*

A spend cube is, on its surface, the dullest object in procurement. Three dimensions — *vendor, category, geography* — sliced into a Rubik's grid of dollars. A finance team will tell you it is a tool for control. A consultant will tell you it is a tool for sourcing strategy. Both are right. But neither has read the book that lives inside it.

## The cube is a novel

Every spend cube is a story being told in fragments. The protagonist is the buyer. The setting is the category. The supporting cast is the supplier panel. The plot tension lives in the variance — the gap between what was budgeted and what was actually paid, between the vendor of record and the vendor doing the work, between the country listed on the contract and the geography where the value is created.

When I open a new spend cube for a category I have never touched before, the first question I ask is not "where can I save?" The first question is, "*who is the protagonist of this story, and what do they want?*"

## Reading variance as plot

Variance is the most underrated signal in procurement. A flat line of spend across twelve months tells you the buyer is on autopilot. A sudden spike in Q3 tells you a new initiative was launched without sourcing involvement. A long tail of small invoices to dozens of suppliers tells you the category is fragmented — and that there is a consolidation play hiding inside.

These are plot points. They demand interpretation, not just calculation.

## The supplier mix as character study

Twenty suppliers in a category does not mean twenty equally important characters. It usually means three protagonists, five supporting players, and twelve walk-on roles that the buyer has been too busy to consolidate. The job of the market intelligence consultant is to identify which is which — and then to write the second act, where the cast is reduced and the remaining suppliers are renegotiated from a position of leverage.

## The brief that reads like fiction

Once I have read the cube as a story, the brief writes itself. Not as a deck of bullet points, but as a narrative: *"In FY25, this category spent X with Y suppliers across Z geographies. The story of this spend is one of fragmentation introduced by a 2023 acquisition that was never integrated into the category strategy. The hero of the next chapter is the consolidated panel of three suppliers, selected for capability and geography. The opportunity is N% — but only if the buyer is willing to write the next chapter herself, rather than letting the spend write it for her."*

A brief like that gets read. A brief like that gets acted on.

The spend cube is not a Rubik's cube. It is a book. Open it.
