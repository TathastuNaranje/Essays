# Cross-Pollination: Composing a sourcing strategy

*Drafted: May 2026*

I learned more about negotiating a multi-supplier global contract from twenty minutes on a fretboard than I did from any procurement textbook. This sounds like an affectation. It is not. The disciplines of music and sourcing share a structural backbone, and once you see it, you cannot unsee it.

## A song is a sourcing strategy

A song has a key. A key constrains the notes you are allowed to play, but it does not dictate which notes you choose. Within the constraint, there is enormous freedom. Outside the constraint, there is only noise.

A sourcing strategy has a key, too. The key is the business outcome — the cost target, the service level, the geographic footprint, the risk tolerance. Every move you make in the negotiation, every concession, every counter-proposal, is a note. If the note is in the key, the song works. If it is out of the key, the deal collapses into noise.

The discipline of music is the discipline of recognizing the key before you play the first note. The discipline of sourcing is the same.

## Practice is the strategy

When I sit down with the guitar in the morning, I am not playing for an audience. I am playing scales, slowly, repeatedly, until my left hand stops thinking about where to go. The audience is invisible. The performance is private. But without that practice, the public performance is impossible.

Sourcing managers do not practice. They prepare for the meeting that is happening tomorrow, then walk into the room with a deck and hope. The best sourcing managers I have worked with practice. They run the negotiation in their heads three times before they run it once with the supplier. They rehearse the objections. They rehearse the silence after they make their offer. They rehearse what they will *not* say.

A scale is boring. A negotiation rehearsal is boring. Both are the work.

## Composition over reaction

The worst sourcing strategies are reactive. The buyer responds to the supplier's price, then responds to the supplier's counter, then responds to the supplier's threat to walk away. This is improvisation without scales — it sounds like noise.

The best sourcing strategies are composed. The buyer writes the song before the meeting. The supplier may bring their own notes, but they are playing inside the buyer's key, on the buyer's tempo, in service of the buyer's structure.

Composition takes longer than reaction. It also produces better music. And better deals.

## The cross-pollination

I do not think procurement teams should hire musicians. I think procurement teams should learn to *think* like musicians — in keys, in scales, in rehearsal, in composition. The cross-pollination is not literal. It is structural.

The next time you walk into a multi-supplier global negotiation, ask yourself: *what key am I in? What is my scale? Have I rehearsed enough? Am I composing, or am I reacting?*

The answer will change how you walk in. It will change how you walk out.
